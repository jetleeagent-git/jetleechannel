En Bloc Thresholds to Be Lowered for Older Condos — What It Means for Owners

4 August 2026 · By Jet Lee · CEA Reg No. R007613B · PropNex Realty

Big news for collective sale watchers today. The Ministry of Law has proposed lower consent thresholds for older developments to secure an en bloc sale — while at the same time strengthening safeguards for owners who don't want to sell.

Here's what's in the Land Titles (Strata) (Amendment) Bill, and what it actually means on the ground.

The Headline Numbers

Development AgeCurrent Consent NeededProposed Consent Needed
Under 10 years90%90% (unchanged)
10 – 39 years80%80% (unchanged)
40 – 59 years80%70%
60 years and above80%65%

That's the core change: for developments aged 40 to 59 years, the threshold drops from 80% to 70%; for those aged 60 and above, it drops from 80% to 65%. Newer developments keep the existing requirements.

MinLaw's reasoning is straightforward — many developments have aged significantly since Singapore's collective sale regime was introduced in 1999. These estates often need substantial investment for maintenance, repairs and upgrading just to stay safe and liveable. Lowering the threshold gives owners of older developments a more practical option to consider redevelopment where there is broad support.

Safeguards Are Also Being Strengthened

This isn't just about making en blocs easier. The Bill tightens several rules to protect owners who don't support a sale:

  • Committee formation threshold raised to 35%: You'll now need 35% of owners (by share value or number of units) to convene a general meeting to form a collective sale committee — up from the current 20% by share value or 25% by number of units.
  • Signature collection window cut to 6 months: Committees get six months instead of twelve to secure signatures for the collective sale agreement. MinLaw says this "addresses concerns about prolonged pressure faced by non-consenting owners."
  • Cooling-off period extended to 3 years: After a failed en bloc attempt, the waiting period before a fresh attempt grows from two years to three — and any new committee formation during that period faces the higher 35% requisition threshold.
  • Bigger compensation pool for objectors: The cap on court-ordered increases to sale proceeds paid to objectors rises to 0.5% of sale proceeds per lot or flat, or S$2,000 per lot or flat, whichever is higher — up from 0.25%.

New Coverage: Long-Leasehold Private Developments

A significant expansion: the amendments extend the collective sale regime to non-strata-titled private residential developments where flat owners hold long leases over their units but don't own the underlying land.

Currently, such developments need unanimous agreement between flat owners and landowners before they can be sold. Under the proposed framework, they could proceed with a majority-consent sale instead — provided flat owners hold leases of at least 850 years. The landowner's interest is then deemed nominal (being subject to leases of that length) and treated as transferred without consideration upon a collective sale order.

What Happens Next

If passed, most amendments apply to ongoing en bloc exercises where the first signature to the collective sale agreement hasn't been obtained before the law comes into force. In estates where the first signature is already in, the existing rules continue to apply.

Committees still collecting signatures can convene general meetings to decide whether to terminate and re-approve terms under the new rules, and get seven months to meet the required consent threshold for a new agreement.

The Bill will be debated by MPs at the next available sitting before the third reading.

My Take: What This Means for Buyers and Owners

1. Older estates just got more en bloc-viable. The gap between 80% and 70% may sound small, but in a 200-unit estate that's 20 fewer signatures needed. For ageing developments with rising maintenance costs, this makes the collective sale route materially more accessible.

2. Expect more redevelopment sites in the pipeline. If these thresholds pass, we'll likely see more older condo sites coming to market over the next few years. That's meaningful for the new launch pipeline — and for buyers, more choice and more benchmark transactions to watch.

3. If you're in an older development, this is the moment to pay attention. Whether you're keen to sell or firmly staying put, the rules of the game are about to change. Knowing your rights — and the new timelines — matters more than ever.
